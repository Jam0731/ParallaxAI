# Source A: founder interview transcript

Recorded for this fictional product's internal content session, 4 September 2026. Interviewer: Jo. Speaker: Alex.

**00:12 — Jo:** What led you to build the review board?

**00:16 — Alex:** On one packaging job I had feedback split between eleven emails and two image attachments. I changed the background colour, but I missed the requested change to the small subtitle. That was my mistake. I needed a way to see which requests were still open before I sent another version.

**00:49 — Jo:** What do you look for when you review work now?

**00:54 — Alex:** I separate a comment from a decision. “Maybe brighter” is something to discuss. “Use version B and keep the current subtitle” tells me what to do next. A board cannot make that decision for a client, but it can keep the decision next to the work.

**01:22 — Jo:** Does that remove the need to talk to a client?

# Source B: FrameBrief product fact sheet

Owner-maintained fact sheet dated 6 September 2026. Applies to the current beta described here.

- B1. FrameBrief is a browser-based review board for image files and PDFs. It accepts uploads of up to 20 MB per file.
- B2. Reviewers can place a comment on a particular image area or PDF page. A comment can be marked open or resolved.
- B3. A project owner can label a version “in review”, “changes requested”, or “approved”. These labels are set by people; they are not automatic decisions.
- B4. A review link lets invited reviewers view and comment without creating an account. The owner chooses which version the link opens.
- B5. The beta does not include time tracking, automatic conflict resolution, or integrations with project-management tools.
- B6. This fact sheet does not include pricing or comparative performance research.

# Source C: Alex's review handoff checklist

Personal working method, written 5 September 2026. These are recommendations I use, not findings from a controlled study.

- C1. Before sending work, write the decision you need from the reviewer. For example: “Please choose the cover image; we are not choosing typefaces in this round.”
- C2. Name the person who will make the final call if comments conflict.
- C3. Put the version name in the message and ask the reviewer to confirm that they are looking at it.
- C4. Before starting a new revision, scan unresolved comments and confirm which requests belong in that revision.

## Script 1 — The tiny change you missed

```text
I changed the background colour and still sent the wrong revision. The change I missed was a small subtitle, buried in feedback across eleven emails and two image attachments. That happened on one packaging job, and it was my mistake.

What I needed was a way to see which requests were still open before I sent another version. That is why I built FrameBrief. In the beta, comments can sit on an image area or a PDF page, and you can mark them open or resolved.

But the useful habit comes before the send button: scan the unresolved comments, then confirm which requests belong in this revision. The big visual change is easy to notice. The small instruction still needs a deliberate check.
```

## Script 2 — “Maybe brighter” is not a decision

```text
“Maybe brighter.” Would you change the design, or ask what that means? If you start adjusting colours now, you could be revising before the client has made a choice.

I separate a comment from a decision. “Maybe brighter” gives us something to discuss. “Use version B and keep the current subtitle” gives me something I can act on.

Before sending work, write the decision you need. For example: “Please choose the cover image; we are not choosing typefaces in this round.” That gives the review a useful boundary.

Then keep the agreed decision beside the work. A board can hold the answer, but it cannot choose for the client. When the comment is unclear, the next step is a clearer question, not another version.
```

**124 words; estimated 50–59 seconds**, at 140–160 words/minute plus 3–6 seconds of pauses. Not a timed read.

Film a talking-head take with two simple on-screen notes: “Maybe brighter” and “Use version B; keep subtitle.” These are example wording, not testimonials. Show two fictional cover options when Alex asks for a choice. Caption moments: “Comment ≠ decision”; “What choice do you need?”; “Ask before revising.”

Sources: A 00:54; C1. The opening conditional consequence and final advice are interpretations of Alex's method, not measured performance claims. “Colours” follows the requested UK locale.

## Script 3 — Two reviewers, two directions

```text
One reviewer says, “Use version A.” Another says, “Use version B.” Putting both comments in one place does not tell you whose decision to follow.

When people want different things, I still need a conversation. Before I begin the next revision, I ask who is making the final call.

There is another check I make before that conversation: which version are we discussing? Put its name in the message and ask the reviewer to confirm they are looking at it.

Those are two separate questions: are we discussing the same work, and who can settle the choice? Once those are clear, confirm the requests that belong in the next revision. A status label can record a decision. It does not make the decision for you.
```

**125 words; estimated 50–60 seconds**, at 140–160 words/minute plus 3–6 seconds of pauses. Not a timed read.

Film Alex beside the fictional tea-shop A/B designs. Label the opening as an example; do not invent a customer disagreement. Add a version name to a mock message and show a simple “Final decision: [agreed role]” teaching card without suggesting a new beta feature. If showing an actual beta status label, demonstrate a person setting it. Caption moments: “Same version?”; “Who makes the final call?”; “A label records a decision.”

Sources: A 01:26; B3, B5; C2–C4. The illustrative opening is a hypothetical version of the documented conflict problem, not Alex claiming another real incident.
